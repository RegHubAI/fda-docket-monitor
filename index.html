About the program: Operation TrialBlazer & the Expedited IND Pilot

Operation TrialBlazer, announced by HHS on June 22, 2026, is the administration's push to keep early-stage drug development anchored in the United States. FDA frames the effort against a competitive backdrop: in 2021 China overtook the U.S. in global share of Phase 1 clinical trials, and from 2020–2025 the eleven largest pharmaceutical companies spent over $150 billion accessing early drug assets developed in China. The initiative pairs this Expedited IND pilot RFI with practical IND infrastructure — Phase 1 CMC clarifications, an IND Navigator page, and a Phase 1 Contact Center — all aimed at compressing the path from drug identification to first-in-human (FIH) study.

The pilot itself would stand up a network of Qualified Research Institutions (QRIs) — academic medical centers, healthcare networks, CROs, regulatory advisors, and similar organizations — that partner with sponsors to develop and pre-review the pharmacology/toxicology, clinical, and CMC components of Phase 1 INDs. Three mechanisms are being tested: raising submission quality to reduce Phase 1 clinical holds; a rolling submission platform that lets FDA review completed IND components as they arrive (with the 30-day review clock starting only when the final component lands, and a "safe to proceed" potentially issued sooner); and running trial-startup activities such as IRB review and site contracting in parallel with IND development. QRIs advise only — FDA retains full regulatory authority, including clinical holds and inspections, and sponsors keep ownership of their INDs. If the pilot succeeds, FDA expects to refine its IND review process more broadly; participation is voluntary, and after the pilot sponsors would pay QRIs directly for their services.

Source: RFI notice, 91 FR 37996 (Jun 24, 2026), § I Background and § II.A Proposed Pilot Structure.

Prepared remarks

  • Opening remarks (Office of the Commissioner): unprecedented urgency and alignment — small companies and capital now choose between the U.S., Australia, and China, not between U.S. cities. TrialBlazer spans idea-to-FIH speed, trial activation, FIH-to-NDA/BLA, and U.S. site/patient participation — but protection of research participants "cannot change."
  • Competitive landscape: the U.S. has lost ~10% of its global Phase 1 share since 2015 while China accelerated; FDA cited a National Security Commission assessment that, as of 2025, the U.S. has ~3 years to reverse the trend before offshoring becomes irreversible. FDA is studying China's investigator-initiated-trial pathway and Australia's HREC-based Clinical Trial Notification scheme.
  • IND requirements (CDER): three stages — pre-IND, IND review, post-IND activation. "Phase-appropriate" is not a lower safety bar; it means asking only for what a Phase 1 safety evaluation needs. Workstreams: Phase 1 CMC clarifications (incl. cell/gene-therapy prior knowledge), mAb/oncology nonclinical streamlining, NAMs, QSP and MABEL dose selection, the plausible-mechanism framework, the Phase 1 IND Navigator site, and a dedicated Phase 1 phone/email contact.
  • Pilot structure (Office of New Drugs): sponsors pair with an AMC, CRO, or other organization and apply together; FDA will select diverse pairs across therapeutic areas, QRI types, and sponsor sizes, and will be "very selective." Pilot length varies by participant. At pilot's end, FDA intends a formal QRI qualification process for QRIs that demonstrate FIH acceleration. "Not a new platform" — the pilot is about rolling regulatory advice during pre-IND.
  • Rolling submission (CBER): replaces the single pre-IND meeting (currently 60–70 days to schedule) with ongoing component-by-component engagement; an optimal submission sequence exists; advice is non-binding but FDA "intends to stay true" to it; the 30-day clock still starts on the final component but should be largely pre-reviewed by then.

Q&A highlights

  • QRI qualification criteria (pre-decisional): documented experience across five disciplines — nonclinical (pharm/tox vetting, starting-dose advice), CMC (method development, gap identification, module review), clinical (board-certified MD/DO with Phase 1 design and safety-monitoring experience), clinical pharmacology (PK/PD), and regulatory affairs (integrated timelines, QRI–sponsor coordination). Discipline-specific depth added per program; in-house research infrastructure not required during the pilot; final criteria will consider RFI feedback.
  • "Components" ≠ eCTD modules: rolling-submission components are the CMC, nonclinical, and clinical pieces; pairs agree with FDA upfront on what needs agency feedback so the rolling phase stays tight and expeditious.
  • Pairing & scope: no FDA-curated QRI list; sponsors use existing AMC/CRO relationships — FDA will not matchmake. Pilot limited to commercial INDs and separate from formal PDUFA meeting programs.
  • IRB parallelization: FDA wants to restore the original parallel-review intent — the draft protocol goes to the IRB while the IND is still pending, targeting IRB approval and site contracts near the safe-to-proceed letter; the QRI catalyzes the sponsor/IRB/site work; FDA won't prescribe those relationships.
  • Success metrics: full clinical-hold rate after IND submission; time from IND submission to first patient in; time from a defined pre-IND point to the may-proceed letter; and the quality/completeness of QRI-vetted components.
  • Authority & safety: FDA retains full authority (investigator disqualification, IRB restrictions, GCP inspections, safety reporting, clinical holds) — framed as enabling speed and avoiding conflicts of interest; reviewers to act as "scientific and regulatory partners," not gatekeepers.
